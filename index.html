Insomnia or sleep apnea? Sort the picture before you book CBT-I

Chronic insomnia and sleep apnea can travel together, and they can also mimic each other. This worksheet is a way to organise what you have already noticed, so a clinician can decide whether a CBT-I course, a breathing test, or both should come first. It is not a self-diagnosis.

NHLBI defines chronic insomnia as trouble that occurs three or more nights a week, lasts more than three months, and cannot be fully explained by another health problem. It also lists cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, a regular sleep schedule, and, in some cases, medicines among the things a clinician may recommend. AASM Sleep Education notes that insomnia can be treated effectively with help from a sleep doctor and describes CBT-I components such as stress reduction, relaxation, sleep-hygiene education and sleep-schedule management. MedlinePlus adds that a clinician may do a physical exam to rule out other problems and may recommend a sleep study.

What CBT-I actually is

A guide to how CBT-I for insomnia works describes it as a structured, evidence-based treatment that changes sleep habits, behaviours and thoughts that keep insomnia going. It states that the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends CBT-I as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, and that a typical programme combines sleep restriction, stimulus control, relaxation skills, sleep education and cognitive restructuring. That is a course of work with a trained clinician, not a single tip.

Where apnea can look like insomnia

The comparison page on insomnia versus sleep apnea says that if insomnia is the main problem, CBT-I is generally the recommended first-line approach, and that persistent snoring or breathing pauses should be assessed rather than treated only with a store-bought product. Age and excess weight can raise apnea risk, but the same page notes that apnea occurs across a range of ages and body sizes. If the main story is pauses, gasping, or sleepiness despite enough time in bed, a breathing-focused test may need to come before, or alongside, a CBT-I referral.

A third guide, on central versus obstructive sleep apnea, is worth reading if a past report already used those words. For obstructive apnea, it describes CPAP as a common first-line treatment. For central apnea it says CPAP can help some people, but treatment is often directed at the cause or at specialised breathing support, and that device choice should be prescribed and reviewed by a qualified clinician.

If both columns have ticks, say so clearly at booking. Sleep Clinic Guide is built to help you find a clinic that fits the case, including CBT-I and apnea testing, rather than assuming one path.

Do not stop a prescribed medicine or a prescribed CPAP machine because of this worksheet. Booking CBT-I does not, by itself, rule out apnea, and a normal bedtime routine does not, by itself, treat either condition.
